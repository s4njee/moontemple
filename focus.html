01 /

Scroll, and the lens follows.

This page is a row of sections standing one behind another, each half again as far away as the last. Whatever crosses the middle of the screen is where the lens is focused. Everything nearer or farther is as soft as that lens would make it.

02 /

One focus, two blurs.

The text is blurred by the browser, with CSS. The lights behind it are blurred by the canvas, as discs the shape of the aperture. Both take their size from the same thin-lens model and the same focus distance, so they never disagree.

03 /

Why not blur it all in the canvas?

A canvas cannot read the page's pixels. CSS can blur them, cheaply, and the text stays text: selectable, findable, read aloud. A bright point it can only dim into a smudge. That part is the canvas's: lights it draws itself.

04 /

Only what changes is written.

A fixed CSS blur costs nothing while the page scrolls; one that changes every frame costs about a millisecond of the browser's time. So only the sections on screen are touched, and only when their blur moves by a tenth of a pixel.

05 /

Depth by distance, not by order.

The blur of a section follows one over its distance, as a lens's does. The far sections crowd together, so focusing from the fifth to the sixth barely changes the first; the near ones are far apart.

06 /

Motion you can turn off.

With reduced motion asked for, nothing blurs and the lights hold still. The switch below turns the page's blur off by itself, for anyone who would rather read.

07 /

The far end.

The last section, twenty metres off. Scroll back up and the lens racks home through every section in turn.
